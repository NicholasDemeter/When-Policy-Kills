### 11. Kratom: Legal in 34 States, Banned in 16

#### Historical Origin

Kratom (*Mitragyna speciosa*), a tropical evergreen native to Southeast Asia—primarily Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia—served indigenous laborers for centuries as pain relief, fatigue mitigation, and mild opioid withdrawal management. Leaves were chewed fresh for stimulant effects (low dose, mitragynine-dominant) or brewed into tea for sedation and analgesia (high dose, 7-hydroxymitragynine-dominant). Thailand's government banned kratom in 1943 under opium laws despite minimal abuse documentation—part of broader anti-opioid moral panic targeting tax revenue threats from a free alternative to opium. Kratom arrived in the United States through ethnobotanical import channels around 2004-2007, initially sold in headshops and smoke shops as "natural painkiller" or "opioid withdrawal aid." Unlike MDMA's rave association or LSD's ceremonial gravitas, kratom entered as botanical commodity—marketed to chronic pain patients, opioid recovery communities, and wellness seekers. No medical research tradition preceded its arrival; American users consumed an ethnobotanical import whose effects mimicked pharmaceuticals but escaped scheduling. This dual identity—traditional medicine and legal gray zone substance—shaped its contentious regulatory battle.

#### Cultural Migration

Kratom's linguistic evolution tracks its identity shift from obscure ethnobotanical to mainstream wellness product. Early slang **"thai weed"** (2004-2007) reflected headshop collector origins, emphasizing Southeast Asian provenance. **"K dog"** (2008-2012) emerged as street shorthand during early smoke shop expansion. Clinical-minded users adopted **"mitra"** (2010s) from the alkaloid mitragynine, signaling pharmaceutical framing. By 2012-2015, **"herbal speed"** captured low-dose stimulant effects among shift workers and students. Opioid recovery communities embraced **"God's gift"** (2014-2018) for withdrawal relief, imbuing kratom with quasi-sacred status. High-dose users self-identified consumption as **"poor man's heroin"** (2015-present), acknowledging opioid-like sedation. Current mainstream slang **"kats"** signals normalization among anxiety and depression self-medicators. Demographically, kratom migrated from ethnobotanical collectors (2004-2008) to chronic pain patients (2008-2012) to opioid recovery seekers (2013-2016 peak) to broad wellness consumers (2017-present). Current users split roughly: 60% pain and anxiety management, 25% opioid withdrawal, 15% energy and focus enhancement. Geographic spread followed commercial channels: urban coastal headshops (2004-2010) to nationwide online vendors (2010-2015) to ubiquitous gas stations and smoke shops (2015-present). Peak growth occurred 2018 with over $1 billion in annual sales. Consumption rituals evolved from traditional leaf chewing to tea brewing (imported authenticity) to powder capsules (convenience) to branded extracts differentiated by vein color and strain ("Red Bali," "Green Maeng Da") to pre-measured liquid shots. Unlike ketamine's festival rave use or nitrous oxide's public spectacle, kratom became domesticated, solitary consumption—morning ritual alongside coffee, not social drug. No dancefloor presence, no vendor mafias, no festival medical tents budgeting for kratom casualties.

#### Harm Profile

Kratom's harm profile reveals a substance in regulatory limbo, occupying middle ground between benign botanical and problematic opioid substitute. Fatal overdose risk (3/10, High confidence) remains low—CDC MMWR documents 152 kratom-detected deaths from 2016-2017, but critically, 90% involved fentanyl co-detection, positioning kratom as harm-reduction attempt during the street opioid crisis rather than primary lethality driver. No documented single-agent overdose mechanism exists at recreational doses. Chronic toxicity (6/10, Moderate confidence) centers on hepatotoxicity—case reports document cholestatic hepatitis requiring transplant in extreme cases, though population-level risk remains unclear versus polypharmacy confounding. Addiction potential (7/10, High confidence) matches moderate opioids: 13% of daily users meet DSM withdrawal criteria with opioid-like dependence patterns including myalgias, insomnia, anxiety, and irritability. Acute psychological risk (4/10, Moderate confidence) stays low—low-dose stimulation rarely causes agitation; high-dose sedation predominates. Chronic psychological effects (3/10, Moderate confidence) remain minimal beyond withdrawal dysphoria. Withdrawal severity (7/10, High confidence) rivals low-dose opioids—manageable but uncomfortable enough to drive continued use, creating dependence cycle. Polydrug risk (8/10, Moderate confidence) proves significant: kratom's CYP3A4 inhibition potentiates fentanyl and benzodiazepines, explaining the 90% co-detected overdoses. Dose variability (6/10, Moderate confidence) remains problematic—leaf and powder alkaloid content varies 0.5-2% depending on vendor, strain, and harvest, creating unpredictable effects. Behavioral disinhibition (4/10, High confidence) stays low—sedation reduces violence versus alcohol or cocaine. Cultural normalization risk (6/10, Moderate confidence) runs elevated: legal smoke shop sales combined with "natural alternative" marketing downplays dependence risk, while wellness industry framing obscures opioid-like properties. The unique risk profile stems from dual pharmacology—stimulation at 1-5 grams, sedation at 10+ grams—creating complex escalation patterns unlike single-effect drugs. Compared to the framework: safer than opioids and benzodiazepines on lethality and addiction dimensions, riskier than psychedelics on dependence and polydrug synergy, matching MDMA's liminal profile of moderate harms across multiple dimensions.

#### Classification Incoherence

Kratom remains federally unscheduled despite DEA emergency scheduling attempts in 2016 (withdrawn after 23,000 public comments) and 2018 (failed), creating a 34-state legal, 16-state banned patchwork that exemplifies regulatory chaos. This state-by-state inconsistency contrasts sharply with kratom's moderate harm profile (3-8 range across indicators)—scores comparable to MDMA (3-7 range) and cannabis (1-5 range), both facing Schedule I federal prohibition. The absurdity crystallizes: LSD with 1/10 acute lethality and 1/10 addiction potential faces Schedule I designation as having "no accepted medical use," while kratom with 3/10 lethality and 7/10 addiction potential sells legally in gas stations alongside energy drinks and cigarettes. FDA Commissioner Scott Gottlieb's 2017-2019 campaign framed kratom as "opioid-like danger" justifying import alerts and vendor prosecutions, yet systematically ignored CDC data showing 90% fentanyl co-detection in kratom-associated deaths—evidence suggesting kratom users were self-medicating during the street opioid crisis, not that kratom itself drives mortality. Meanwhile, buprenorphine (Suboxone) requires specialist prescribing under controlled substance regulations despite similar dependence profile (8/10 addiction potential), while kratom faces no federal restrictions. The regulatory absurdity extends to comparison with benzodiazepines: Xanax and Valium, Schedule IV drugs with 9/10 addiction potential and potentially lethal withdrawal, flow freely through prescription channels, while kratom—with lower addiction scores and non-lethal withdrawal—faces state-level prohibition and federal scheduling threats. The unscheduled status paradoxically prevents FDA oversight and quality control: vendors operate in gray markets, alkaloid content varies wildly, contamination risks persist, yet federal inaction maintains this dangerous limbo rather than implementing rational regulation. The true paradox emerges from stakeholder interests: cheaper, accessible withdrawal aid with proven harm-reduction utility gets demonized, while expensive patented pharmaceuticals remain gatekept through bureaucratic X-waiver systems and prescription requirements that limit access to those who could benefit most.

#### Who Benefits from Misclassification

Pharmaceutical interests lead the beneficiary list, protecting an $80 billion pain management market from a cheap herbal competitor requiring no patents, no R&D investment, no prescription infrastructure. Companies like Purdue, Johnson & Johnson, and Teva profit from maintaining opioid prescription flows while blocking botanical alternatives. The treatment industry gatekeeps through specialist-only buprenorphine access—kratom offers over-the-counter withdrawal relief threatening the addiction treatment business model built on mandatory counseling, supervised dosing, and insurance billing. Smoke shops and gas stations thrive on unscheduled status, generating over $1 billion in annual sales through unregulated powder, capsule, and extract sales; state-level bans create scarcity pricing in neighboring jurisdictions. Law enforcement benefits from asset forfeiture in the 16 ban states while maintaining budget justifications through fragmented enforcement—50-state patchwork ensures perpetual confusion and selective prosecution opportunities. Abstinence advocacy groups maintain kratom as ongoing "drug threat" justifying organizational budgets despite 90% fentanyl co-detection evidence undermining primary harm claims. The key winners remain gray market vendors exploiting legal ambiguity—powder and capsules selling for $15-80 per ounce with zero quality control, zero alkaloid standardization, zero contamination screening. FDA warning letters paradoxically boost "natural alternative" branding, driving sales among consumers seeking non-pharmaceutical pain management. Insurance companies avoid covering kratom (unscheduled, no FDA approval), forcing out-of-pocket purchases while covering expensive prescription alternatives. The losers: self-medicators navigating 16-state bans and random import alerts, chronic pain patients denied access to non-addictive alternatives, opioid-dependent individuals lacking harm-reduction tools. The misclassification maintains profitable chaos—vendors profit from legal uncertainty, pharma protects market share, treatment centers preserve patient pipelines, law enforcement justifies budgets—while users develop dependence on unstandardized botanical products that could, under rational regulation, serve as bridge therapy away from deadly fentanyl-contaminated street opioids.
### 12. Suboxone: Life-Saving Strips or Street Currency?

#### Historical Origin

Buprenorphine, a partial mu-opioid agonist synthesized in 1969, remained medical curiosity until the 2002 FDA approval of Suboxone—buprenorphine combined with naloxone to deter injection abuse. The combination represented rational pharmaceutical design: high-affinity receptor binding blocks full agonists like heroin and fentanyl, ceiling effect on respiratory depression prevents overdose, naloxone component triggers precipitated withdrawal if crushed and injected, theoretically limiting diversion potential. Suboxone emerged amid post-OxyContin crisis wreckage when Purdue Pharma's aggressive marketing had created millions of opioid-dependent Americans. The Drug Addiction Treatment Act of 2000 enabled office-based opioid treatment, breaking methadone clinic monopoly—qualified physicians could prescribe buprenorphine after obtaining DEA X-waiver certification limiting patient loads (30, then 100, then 275). This created specialist bottleneck: by 2020, only 10% of physicians held X-waivers despite overwhelming patient need. Initial context positioned Suboxone as harm-reduction triumph—outpatient maintenance without clinic requirements, lower abuse liability than methadone, medication-assisted treatment expanding beyond specialty centers. Early diversion emerged immediately, however—street prices reached $1 per milligram versus $8-10 retail, creating black market from day one. The X-waiver system simultaneously enabled treatment expansion and created artificial scarcity driving diversion markets, establishing dual identity that persists: legitimate life-saving medication and illicit street opioid substitute.

#### Cultural Migration

Suboxone's scene evolution reveals liminal space between medical treatment and street economy. Clinical shorthand **"bupe"** (2002-2010) dominated among physicians and recovery program staff. **"Strips"** (2006-present) referenced sublingual film delivery format versus earlier tablets. **"Orange guys"** (2008-2015) nodded distinctive Suboxone film color. Street slang evolved separately: **"subs"** (2010s general street term), **"matty ice"** (Suboxone-methadone combination), **"bupe boys"** (rural diversion dealers specializing in Suboxone sales). The street ecology operated distinctly from recreational drug markets—Suboxone functioned as heroin substitute during supply disruptions, with strips sold at $1 per milligram street price versus $8-10 retail, creating diversion premium. Rural prescription mills, pharmacy theft, and international export (Europe, Australia) fed black markets. The X-waiver era (2002-2021) created specialist bottleneck—only 10% of physicians certified, driving diversion premium as patients facing treatment waitlists turned to street sources. Post-2021 deregulation (X-waiver eliminated, any MD/NP can prescribe) slashed street prices 70% but diversion ecology persists through pharmacy theft and doctor shopping. Community perspectives split sharply: recovery communities revere Suboxone as "life-saver" citing 50% overdose reduction in maintenance patients; active opioid users cycle on and off during fentanyl shortages; dealers treat as reliable commodity with steady demand. Critically, Suboxone maintains zero festival or rave footprint—this remains strictly opioid ecosystem drug, not recreational substance. Consumption rituals evolved from supervised clinic dosing to home sublingual administration to crushed-and-snorted strips (bypassing naloxone barrier through nasal absorption). Geographic patterns centered on rural Appalachia (heroin epicenter) with urban export hubs feeding secondary markets.

#### Harm Profile

Suboxone's harm profile embodies treatment paradox—designed for safety yet generating complex risks through misuse and diversion. Acute lethality (2/10, Moderate confidence) remains low due to ceiling effect preventing respiratory depression when used alone—rare solo overdose deaths occur, primarily in opioid-naive individuals. However, high dependence potential (8/10, High confidence) maintains physical addiction, which constitutes therapeutic requirement but complicates risk profile. Chronic toxicity (5/10, Moderate confidence) includes dental decay from acidic sublingual films (FDA black box warning 2022) and rare hepatotoxicity cases. Acute psychological risk (3/10, Moderate confidence) stays minimal—sedation predominates, no stimulant agitation or dissociative confusion. Chronic psychological effects (2/10, Low confidence) remain absent beyond baseline opioid use disorder. Withdrawal severity (6/10, Moderate confidence) presents unique challenge: precipitated withdrawal occurs if Suboxone taken during active full-agonist dependence (buprenorphine's high affinity displaces heroin/fentanyl, triggering immediate severe withdrawal), while standard Suboxone withdrawal proves protracted but less intense than full agonists. Polydrug risk (9/10, High confidence) rivals benzodiazepines—benzodiazepine synergy proves lethal despite opioid ceiling effect because naloxone doesn't block GABA receptors. CDC data shows most buprenorphine deaths involve benzos or alcohol. Dose variability remains low (4/10, Moderate confidence)—pharmaceutical-grade standardization ensures consistency, though street diversion strips vary in handling (degradation, contamination). Behavioral disinhibition (3/10, High confidence) stays minimal—sedation reduces violence risk. Cultural normalization risk (5/10, Moderate confidence) splits: "treatment" framing obscures that 20-30% of prescriptions divert to street markets per DEA estimates, while recovery narratives celebrate life-saving properties. Unique risks include nasal damage from crushed strips, insurance barriers delaying legitimate access and creating heroin-use gaps, and diversion economy funding rural opioid markets. Compared to framework: less lethal than heroin/fentanyl and benzodiazepines, more dependence and polydrug risk than ketamine or MDMA, matching benzodiazepines as pharmaceutical profit center maintaining dependence under medical guise. Core paradox: proven overdose reducer (unlike benzos' pure harm) creates moral complexity absent in purely recreational catastrophic drugs—Suboxone saves lives while simultaneously feeding diversion markets.

#### Classification Incoherence

Suboxone's Schedule III status—requiring specialist prescribing until 2021 despite moderate harm profile (2-9 range across indicators)—demonstrates how regulatory architecture can obstruct public health. The substance exhibits higher polydrug risk (9/10) and dependence potential (8/10) than ketamine (Schedule III, broader prescribing access) or cannabis (Schedule I despite 4/10 dependence). The absurdity crystallizes through historical comparison: X-waiver system (2002-2021) limited prescribers to 10% of physicians despite documented 50% overdose reduction in maintenance patients, driving $1 per milligram diversion market as patients facing treatment waitlists turned to street sources. Compare Suboxone's gatekeeping to alcohol: alcohol with 9/10 acute lethality and 9/10 addiction potential sells freely, while Suboxone with 2/10 lethality required specialist certification blocking access for two decades. Post-2021 deregulation produced 400% prescription increase, yet specialty addiction clinics pivot to "holistic MAT" packages maintaining gatekeeping through counseling requirements and insurance barriers. Within Schedule III, Suboxone faced tighter restrictions than ketamine or anabolic steroids—drugs lacking similar public health benefit. The scheduling classification assumes diversion risk outweighs treatment benefit, yet methadone (Schedule II, higher overdose risk) receives government clinic support while Suboxone faced bureaucratic barriers. Most perversely, Reckitt Benckiser (original Suboxone patent holder) earned over $2.5 billion from monopoly pricing charging $500+ monthly per patient pre-generic entry, exploiting restricted access that artificial scarcity enabled. Insurance companies favored 30-day prescriptions over urgent access, creating gaps where patients relapsed to fentanyl-contaminated heroin. The classification reveals pharmaceutical pricing paradox: life-saving medication with proven efficacy subjected to more barriers than dangerous recreational drugs due to corporate profit optimization combined with abstinence-only stigma. True risk-based scheduling would recognize Suboxone as Schedule IV medication (low abuse potential when properly prescribed, accepted medical use), removing barriers while maintaining appropriate prescribing oversight—current Schedule III designation with former X-waiver requirements prioritized diversion prevention over overdose prevention, killing through access restriction.

#### Who Benefits from Misclassification

Reckitt Benckiser (original patent holder) leads the beneficiary list, earning over $2.5 billion from monopoly pricing at $500+ monthly per patient before generic competition emerged. The X-waiver bottleneck (2002-2021) artificially restricted supply, enabling premium pricing—Suboxone films cost pennies to produce yet commanded pharmaceutical-grade prices due to limited prescriber pool. Specialty addiction clinics profit from mandatory counseling and supervised dosing requirements—X-waiver certification required training but created captive patient population. These clinics bill for counseling, urinalysis, and "comprehensive MAT packages" generating thousands per patient annually. Post-2021 deregulation threatened this model, prompting specialty centers to rebrand as "holistic MAT" providers requiring extensive wraparound services (at premium prices) despite evidence that medication alone reduces overdose mortality. The diversion economy itself generates revenue: street prices at $1 per milligram versus $8 retail created arbitrage opportunities for dealers and patients selling portions of prescriptions to fund other needs. Rural heroin markets depend on Suboxone availability when fentanyl supply tightens—dealers stockpile strips as reliable product. DEA and pharmaceutical industry collude through diversion panic rhetoric: DEA justifies enforcement budgets through buprenorphine "crisis" warnings while pharma cites diversion to justify restrictive distribution. Insurance companies benefit from barriers—prior authorization requirements, mandatory counseling prerequisites, and 30-day script limits generate administrative revenue while rationing expensive medication. Law enforcement agencies profit from Suboxone-related arrests (prescription fraud, pharmacy theft, illegal distribution) maintaining budgets through treatment medication enforcement. Most cynically, stigma maintenance serves abstinence advocacy groups opposing medication-assisted treatment as "trading one addiction for another"—this ideology, contradicted by evidence of 50% mortality reduction, preserves treatment industry models requiring expensive long-term residential care over evidence-based maintenance. The losers remain opioid-dependent individuals facing treatment waitlists, insurance denials, and stigma while fentanyl-contaminated street supply kills 100,000+ annually. The misclassification transforms proven public health intervention into profit center—pharmaceutical companies extract billions, specialty clinics maintain patient monopolies, DEA justifies enforcement spending—while access barriers drive preventable deaths, all under the guise of preventing diversion that medication restriction itself creates through artificial scarcity.
### 13. Tramadol: Sold as 'Non-Narcotic,' Delivered Seizures

#### Historical Origin

Tramadol emerged from German pharmaceutical company Grünenthal's laboratories in 1962 as "atypical centrally-acting analgesic"—dual mechanism combining weak mu-opioid agonism with SNRI (serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibition) properties. This hybrid pharmacology positioned tramadol between pure opioids and antidepressants, creating unique profile marketed as safer alternative. FDA approval arrived in 1995 under brand name Ultram, crucially marketed as "non-narcotic" despite opioid activity—the SNRI component allowed Janssen Pharmaceuticals (US marketer) to emphasize non-traditional mechanism while downplaying addiction potential. This "non-narcotic" label proved devastatingly effective: doctors prescribed freely for moderate-severe pain, elderly patients received it as "gentler" than traditional opioids, and regulatory scrutiny remained minimal. Tramadol remained unscheduled until 2014 when DEA finally imposed Schedule IV classification after nearly two decades of mounting abuse evidence—dependence cases, emergency room visits, and addiction treatment admissions demonstrated that "atypical" didn't mean "safe." The 19-year delay from launch to scheduling represents one of pharmaceutical industry's most successful marketing triumphs: Janssen and Johnson & Johnson generated over $10 billion in revenue from a drug whose "non-narcotic" branding obscured opioid-like dependence potential, creating prescription pipeline that continues with 300+ million annual scripts in 2024. The delayed classification exposed regulatory capture—evidence of harm accumulated while pharmaceutical profits soared, with scheduling action arriving only after generic competition emerged and brand-name revenue declined.

#### Cultural Migration

Tramadol's evolution reveals prescription opioid ecosystem rather than recreational drug culture. Early slang **"trammies," "roids," "ultras"** (US 1995-2010) referenced brand name and pill form without recreational mystique—this remained medical commodity, not party drug. **"Hillbilly heroin lite"** (2005-2015) captured its role as OxyContin substitution during prescription crackdown, acknowledging opioid effects despite "non-narcotic" marketing. International nomenclature diverged dramatically: **"tram-doll"** and **"phone pills"** (Nigeria 2010-present) emerged as West Africa experienced tramadol explosion—the drug became cheapest heroin alternative at $0.10 per pill versus $1+ for heroin, prompting UN warnings of "tramadol crisis" across the Sahel by 2017. The prescription cascade tracked American pain management evolution: elderly chronic pain patients (1995-2005) as initial targets benefiting from less constipation than traditional opioids, post-surgical patients (2000s) receiving tramadol as discharge medication, OxyContin crisis substitution (2008-2014 peak diversion) as doctors shifted from Schedule II opioids to supposedly safer Schedule IV alternative (pre-2014 unscheduled status), and ultimately international export hubs with Nigeria and Egypt becoming trafficking centers. Tramadol maintained zero festival or rave footprint—this remained pure prescription opioid ecosystem, not recreational scene. By 2010s, street ecology developed: prescription mills in Florida and Appalachia, port diversion to African markets, pharmacy theft feeding local black markets. Consumption rituals stayed pharmaceutical: oral tablets for legitimate pain management, crushed and snorted for faster euphoria among recreational users. The serotonin syndrome awareness shift marked critical cultural change—pre-2010 medical mythology of "non-narcotic safety" gave way to post-2014 FDA black box warnings requiring SSRI polypharmacy avoidance, fundamentally altering prescribing practices. Demographically, tramadol never achieved single identity: elderly patients consuming legitimately, working-age chronic pain sufferers navigating dependence, international street markets replacing heroin—each population experiencing identical pharmacology through vastly different access channels.

#### Harm Profile

Tramadol's harm profile exposes "safe opioid" deception through unique risk constellation. Moderate acute lethality (5/10, Moderate confidence) stems from dual mechanism—serotonin syndrome combined with seizures at supratherapeutic doses accounts for approximately 1,000 deaths annually. Unlike pure opioids killing through respiratory depression, tramadol kills through serotonergic crisis: hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, seizures. Deaths cluster among polypharmacy users combining tramadol with SSRIs, creating serotonin toxicity fatal within hours. Chronic toxicity (5/10, Moderate confidence) remains debated—serotonin neurotoxicity concerns persist alongside dependence complications including cognitive impairment. Addiction potential (7/10, High confidence) affects 10-15% of chronic users, developing through combined SNRI and weak mu-opioid effects creating physical dependence. Acute psychological risk (5/10, Moderate confidence) includes serotonin syndrome presenting as psychiatric emergency—agitation, hallucinations, confusion—distinguishing tramadol from pure opioids' sedative profile. Chronic psychological effects (3/10, Moderate confidence) appear confounded by withdrawal phenomena. Withdrawal severity (6/10, Moderate confidence) combines opioid withdrawal (myalgias, insomnia) with SSRI discontinuation syndrome (electric shock sensations, mood instability), creating protracted discomfort driving relapse. Polydrug risk (8/10, Moderate confidence) runs high: SSRI and SNRI synergy creates serotonin syndrome, benzodiazepine combination lowers seizure threshold further, alcohol potentiates CNS depression. Dose variability (5/10, Moderate confidence) stems from prescription diversion—street tramadol's inconsistent storage and handling affects potency. Behavioral disinhibition (5/10, High confidence) falls intermediate—neither pure sedation nor stimulation, tramadol produces moderate impulsivity placing violence risk between depressants and stimulants. Cultural normalization risk (6/10, Moderate confidence) persists through "non-narcotic" marketing legacy—despite Schedule IV classification and black box warnings, both medical professionals and patients retain outdated safety assumptions, driving continued over-prescription. Unique risks distinguish tramadol: seizure threshold lowering occurs even at therapeutic doses in susceptible individuals, neonatal abstinence syndrome affects babies born to tramadol-dependent mothers, and serotonin syndrome can prove fatal within hours of polypharmacy combination. Compared to framework: less acutely lethal than cocaine (8/10) or opioids (10/10), higher acute psychological risk than suboxone (3/10), matching MDMA's neurotoxicity debates and liminal risk profile. Core paradox: "weak opioid" mythology drove 300 million annual prescriptions creating dependence epidemic rivaling OxyContin pre-crackdown, yet tramadol retains safer reputation despite evidence.
